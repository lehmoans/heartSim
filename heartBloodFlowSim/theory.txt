HEART BLOOD-FLOW SIMULATION — THEORY NOTES
==========================================

Repository: lehmoans/heartSim
Location: heartBloodFlowSim/theory.txt

Purpose
-------
This document collects the mathematical and physical theory needed to understand the
educational immersed-boundary (IB) heart-flow simulator. It is intended as a study
document. Equations are written in Word Equation Editor linear-input form: in Word,
press Alt+= to create an equation, then paste the equation line. Use the surrounding
text for definitions and assumptions.

MODEL SCOPE AND FIDELITY
-----------------------
The project is a learning implementation inspired by:

C. S. Peskin (1977), "Numerical Analysis of Blood Flow in the Heart",
Journal of Computational Physics, 25, 220–252.
DOI: 10.1016/0021-9991(77)90100-0.

The core model is a two-dimensional, incompressible, Newtonian fluid on a fixed
Cartesian Eulerian grid. Moving heart-wall and valve structures are represented by
Lagrangian marker points connected by mechanical links. A regularised delta kernel
transfers force from the moving structure to the fluid grid and transfers fluid
velocity back to the structure.

This repository is a teaching implementation, not a complete reproduction of every
equation, geometry, parameter, discretisation, or result in Peskin (1977). In
particular, simple spring laws, a triangular hat kernel, and a periodic Fourier
Poisson demonstration are pedagogical choices. They must not be described as the
exact original paper's implementations.

The first objective is to understand and validate the separate mathematical
components. A realistic cardiac cycle, realistic tissue constitutive laws, full
valve geometry, source/sink circulation model, and fully validated fluid-structure
interaction (FSI) are later extensions.

======================================================================
1. PHYSICAL DESCRIPTION AND CONTINUUM ASSUMPTIONS
======================================================================

1.1 What is being modelled?

Blood is treated initially as a Newtonian fluid with constant density and dynamic
viscosity. This is a useful first approximation for understanding flow and numerical
coupling. Real blood can exhibit non-Newtonian behaviour, especially in small vessels
and low-shear conditions; that complexity is outside the initial model.

The fluid occupies a time-dependent region, but the computational fluid grid remains
fixed. The heart wall and valve leaflets move through this grid. Their mechanical
action is represented as a force applied to the fluid.

Initial assumptions:
- Constant fluid density rho.
- Constant dynamic viscosity mu.
- Incompressible flow.
- Newtonian constitutive behaviour.
- Two spatial dimensions for the initial implementation.
- Fluid solved on a fixed Cartesian grid.
- Tissue represented by Lagrangian points and links.
- No turbulence model in the initial low-complexity solver.
- Gravity may be omitted initially; if included, it is a body-force term.
- Boundary conditions and geometry are simplified for individual numerical tests.

1.2 Eulerian and Lagrangian descriptions

Eulerian description:
The fluid velocity and pressure are described at fixed spatial coordinates.

u(x,t) = fluid velocity at position x and time t
p(x,t) = fluid pressure at position x and time t

Lagrangian description:
The immersed tissue is described by material coordinates s. A material point moves
through physical space and has position:

X(s,t) = position of tissue point labelled by s at time t

The boundary velocity is:

U_b(s,t) = partial X(s,t) / partial t

The distinction matters because the fluid grid does not move with the tissue, while
the Lagrangian markers do.

======================================================================
2. CONSERVATION LAWS AND NAVIER–STOKES EQUATIONS
======================================================================

2.1 Conservation of mass

The general compressible continuity equation is:

partial rho / partial t + div(rho u) = 0

For constant density, this reduces to:

div(u) = 0

In Cartesian coordinates in two dimensions:

partial u_x / partial x + partial u_y / partial y = 0

Physical meaning:
For an incompressible fluid, a small fluid element does not change its volume. Fluid
cannot accumulate locally through compression. The velocity field must therefore
have zero divergence.

Divergence is a measure of local volumetric expansion or contraction:
- Positive divergence: local expansion.
- Negative divergence: local compression.
- Zero divergence: locally volume-preserving flow.

2.2 Conservation of momentum

Newton's second law applied to a continuum gives the momentum equation. For a
constant-density Newtonian incompressible fluid with an immersed force density:

rho [ partial u / partial t + (u dot grad)u ]
    = -grad(p) + mu Laplacian(u) + F

In Word Equation Editor:

\rho\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}
+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\right)
=-\nabla p+\mu\nabla^2\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{F}

Together with:

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0

Definitions:
rho = fluid density [kg/m^3]
u = fluid velocity vector [m/s]
t = time [s]
p = pressure [Pa]
mu = dynamic viscosity [Pa s]
F = immersed force per unit volume [N/m^3]
grad(p) = pressure gradient
Laplacian(u) = spatial diffusion of velocity
(u dot grad)u = convective acceleration

Every term in the momentum equation has units of force per unit volume:

rho * acceleration = (kg/m^3)(m/s^2) = N/m^3

Pressure-gradient units:
Pa/m = (N/m^2)/m = N/m^3

Viscous term units:
mu * (velocity / length^2)
= (Pa s)(m/s/m^2) = N/m^3

2.3 Meaning of each momentum term

A. Local acceleration:

partial u / partial t

Describes how velocity changes with time at a fixed location.

B. Convective acceleration:

(u dot grad)u

Describes how a moving fluid particle experiences changes in velocity because it
travels through regions with different velocity. In 2D:

(u dot grad)u =
u_x * partial u / partial x + u_y * partial u / partial y

C. Pressure force:

-grad(p)

Pressure differences drive and redistribute fluid momentum. In an incompressible
solver, pressure also acts as the constraint force that adjusts velocity so that the
discrete velocity field is divergence-free.

D. Viscous diffusion:

mu * Laplacian(u)

Viscosity transports momentum between neighbouring fluid regions and resists
velocity gradients. For constant mu and incompressible Newtonian flow, the viscous
term takes this simple Laplacian form.

E. Immersed force density:

F

Represents the force exerted on the fluid by the immersed heart wall, valve leaflets,
or other modelled structures. It is not an additional pressure term. It is a force
per unit volume distributed over nearby Eulerian grid cells.

2.4 Component equations in 2D

Let u = (u,v), with x and y coordinates. The incompressible continuity equation is:

partial u / partial x + partial v / partial y = 0

The x-momentum equation is:

rho [ partial u/partial t + u partial u/partial x
      + v partial u/partial y ]
= -partial p/partial x
  + mu [ partial^2 u/partial x^2 + partial^2 u/partial y^2 ]
  + F_x

The y-momentum equation is:

rho [ partial v/partial t + u partial v/partial x
      + v partial v/partial y ]
= -partial p/partial y
  + mu [ partial^2 v/partial x^2 + partial^2 v/partial y^2 ]
  + F_y

These are the equations the fluid solver must approximate numerically.

2.5 Kinematic viscosity

Kinematic viscosity is dynamic viscosity divided by density:

nu = mu / rho

Units:

[m^2/s]

It is useful because dividing the momentum equation by density gives:

partial u/partial t + (u dot grad)u
= -(1/rho) grad(p) + nu Laplacian(u) + F/rho

2.6 Illustrative blood-like values

The notebook currently uses illustrative values:

rho = 1060 kg/m^3
mu = 0.0035 Pa s
nu = mu/rho

These are demonstration inputs, not parameters fitted to Peskin (1977), nor a
patient-specific blood model. The physical parameter set should be documented and
checked before interpreting physiological results.

======================================================================
3. NON-DIMENSIONALISATION AND FLOW REGIMES
======================================================================

Non-dimensionalisation compares the relative importance of physical effects.

Choose characteristic length L, velocity U, and time L/U.

The Reynolds number is:

Re = rho U L / mu = U L / nu

Interpretation:
- Low Re: viscous effects are relatively important.
- High Re: inertial effects are relatively important.
- Re does not alone determine whether a flow is turbulent; geometry, disturbances,
  and unsteadiness also matter.

The unsteady Womersley number is useful for pulsatile internal flow:

alpha = R * sqrt(omega * rho / mu)

where:
R = characteristic vessel or chamber length scale (often radius)
omega = angular frequency of pulsation [rad/s]

It compares unsteady inertia with viscous diffusion in oscillatory flow. It is a
useful later diagnostic for pulsatile cardiovascular flow, but it is not required
for the first numerical implementation.

For periodic motion:

omega = 2*pi*f

where f is frequency in cycles per second.

======================================================================
4. IMMERSED-BOUNDARY METHOD: CORE IDEA
======================================================================

4.1 Why an immersed-boundary method?

A heart wall and valve leaflets move and deform. A body-fitted fluid mesh would need
to deform or be regenerated as these structures move. This can be difficult when
surfaces undergo large deformation or interact with the flow.

The immersed-boundary method avoids moving the fluid mesh:
- Fluid variables remain on a fixed Eulerian Cartesian grid.
- Tissue is represented by moving Lagrangian markers.
- Tissue forces are spread to nearby fluid cells.
- Fluid velocity is interpolated back to the tissue markers.
- The two systems exchange information at every time step.

This is a fluid-structure interaction method: the fluid affects tissue motion and
tissue forces affect the fluid.

4.2 Boundary force spreading

Let f(s,t) be force per unit Lagrangian coordinate acting on the fluid through the
immersed boundary. The Eulerian force density is:

F(x,t) = integral f(s,t) delta_h(x - X(s,t)) ds

Word equation form:

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},t)
=\int \mathbf{f}(s,t)\,\delta_h(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{X}(s,t))\,ds

The regularised delta function is centred at each boundary point. It distributes
that point's force over a small neighbourhood of the fixed fluid grid.

The result F(x,t) is a force density, not a point force.

4.3 Velocity interpolation

The boundary point moves with the local fluid velocity interpolated from the grid:

partial X(s,t)/partial t
= integral u(x,t) delta_h(x - X(s,t)) dx

Word equation form:

\frac{\partial\mathbf{X}(s,t)}{\partial t}
=\int\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)
\delta_h(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{X}(s,t))\,d\mathbf{x}

This is the no-slip coupling condition in the IB formulation: the immersed boundary
is carried by the fluid velocity evaluated at the boundary.

For a prescribed-motion test, X(s,t) may instead be specified directly. That is a
one-way or prescribed-kinematics test, not fully two-way fluid-structure coupling.

4.4 Regularised delta function

The mathematical Dirac delta is singular. A numerical method uses a smooth,
grid-dependent approximation delta_h with compact support.

In multiple dimensions, a common tensor-product form is:

delta_h(x) = (1/h^d) product over dimensions of phi(x_j/h)

For 2D:

delta_h(x,y) = (1/h^2) phi(x/h) phi(y/h)

Here h is grid spacing and phi is a one-dimensional kernel.

The current teaching notebook uses the triangular hat kernel:

phi(r) = max(1 - |r|, 0)

It has compact support over |r| < 1. It is simple for demonstrating interpolation
and spreading, but it is not claimed to be the exact improved kernel used in the
original paper.

4.5 Discrete force spreading

For a uniform 2D grid with spacing h and boundary markers indexed by k:

F_ij = sum_k f_k delta_h(x_ij - X_k) Delta s_k

where Delta s_k is the Lagrangian quadrature weight or marker spacing, depending on
how the discrete boundary force is defined.

For a single point force in 2D, a simple illustrative expression is:

F_ij = F_b * w_ij / h^2

where w_ij is the dimensionless kernel weight. The exact scale must be consistent
with the units and definition of the marker force.

4.6 Discrete velocity interpolation

For marker k:

U_k = sum_ij u_ij delta_h(x_ij - X_k) h^2

If the kernel is normalised correctly, interpolation of a constant velocity field
should return that same constant velocity.

4.7 Transfer properties to test

Partition of unity:
The discrete interpolation weights should sum to one:

sum_ij delta_h(x_ij - X_k) h^2 = 1

Constant preservation:
If u_ij = U_0 at all grid points, then:

U_k = U_0

Force conservation:
The integral of the spread force density should equal the total boundary force,
up to quadrature and discretisation error:

integral F(x,t) dx = integral f(s,t) ds

Power consistency:
With compatible spreading and interpolation operators, the work done by the
boundary force on the fluid should match the work done by the corresponding
Lagrangian force against the interpolated boundary velocity, up to discretisation
details. This is an important structural property of IB schemes.

======================================================================
5. TISSUE MECHANICS: LINKS, SPRINGS, AND BOUNDARY FORCES
======================================================================

5.1 Lagrangian marker network

The immersed wall or leaflet is represented by points X_k. Links connect selected
pairs of points. A link network defines how the structure resists deformation.

A link may represent local stretching resistance. A closed network can approximate
a membrane or boundary curve. The connectivity is fixed even though marker
positions change.

5.2 Geometry of one link

For endpoints A and B:

d = X_B - X_A
L = |d|
q = d/L

where:
d = link vector
L = current link length
q = unit vector along the link

Let L_0 be the resting length.

5.3 Linear spring law

A simple teaching model uses a linear spring with stiffness k_s:

F_A = k_s (L - L_0) q
F_B = -F_A

The sign convention above makes a stretched link pull its endpoints together.
If L > L_0, the force on A points towards B and the force on B points towards A.

The forces are equal and opposite:

F_A + F_B = 0

This is internal force balance. A link cannot create net force on the entire
isolated structure.

The spring potential energy is:

E_s = (1/2) k_s (L - L_0)^2

The magnitude of the spring force is:

|F_s| = k_s |L - L_0|

Units of stiffness depend on the force definition. For a discrete point spring,
k_s has units N/m. For a continuum membrane tension, units and quadrature factors
must be defined consistently.

5.4 Multiple links

For marker k connected to neighbouring markers, the net elastic force is the sum
of contributions from all connected links:

F_k = sum over links incident on k of F_(link,k)

The full boundary force vector is assembled from all marker forces.

A network can still have unstable deformation modes even if each individual spring
law is restoring. Stability depends on topology, geometry, stiffness, time
integration, and how the boundary is coupled to the fluid.

5.5 Limits of a spring-only tissue model

A simple spring network is not a complete cardiac tissue constitutive model.
Real myocardium is anisotropic, nonlinear, active, and viscoelastic. Valve tissue
also has nonlinear anisotropic mechanical behaviour.

Later improvements may include:
- Nonlinear tension-length relationships.
- Bending resistance.
- Area or volume constraints.
- Viscoelastic damping.
- Anisotropic fibre directions.
- Active muscle contraction.
- Separate constitutive models for myocardium and valve leaflets.

These should be added only after the simpler link mechanics are tested.

5.6 Prescribed motion versus force-driven motion

Prescribed motion:
The boundary location X(s,t) is imposed as a known function of time. This is useful
for testing fluid response to a moving wall, but does not predict the wall motion.

Force-driven motion:
The boundary position is determined by the interaction of fluid forces, tissue
elasticity, active muscle forces, and possibly structural inertia. This is a more
complete FSI problem and requires a boundary mechanics model.

The project should keep these two modes conceptually separate.

======================================================================
6. INCOMPRESSIBLE FLOW AND THE PRESSURE POISSON EQUATION
======================================================================

6.1 Why pressure is a constraint variable

In incompressible flow, density is constant and velocity must satisfy:

div(u) = 0

Pressure adjusts so that the velocity field obeys this constraint. In a projection
method, the velocity is first advanced without the final pressure correction. A
pressure field is then calculated and used to remove the divergent component.

6.2 Provisional velocity

A generic explicit momentum predictor can be written:

u* = u^n + Delta t [
    - (u^n dot grad)u^n
    + nu Laplacian(u^n)
    + F^n/rho
]

This compact expression omits pressure in the predictor. Exact time discretisation,
operator placement, and treatment of the immersed force depend on the selected
scheme. It is a conceptual modern projection-method form, not a claim that it is
identical to every detail of Peskin's 1977 algorithm.

6.3 Pressure correction

Correct provisional velocity using pressure:

u^(n+1) = u* - (Delta t/rho) grad(p)

Take divergence:

div(u^(n+1)) = div(u*) - (Delta t/rho) Laplacian(p)

Impose incompressibility:

div(u^(n+1)) = 0

Therefore:

Laplacian(p) = (rho/Delta t) div(u*)

Word equation form:

\nabla^2 p=\frac{\rho}{\Delta t}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}^*

This is the pressure Poisson equation.

6.4 Why solving Poisson enforces incompressibility

The pressure solution is chosen so that its gradient removes the divergence in the
provisional velocity. Substituting the pressure equation into the corrected
velocity's divergence gives zero in the continuous derivation.

In a numerical method, the result depends on discrete operator compatibility.
The discrete divergence, gradient, and Laplacian must be chosen consistently.

6.5 Discrete projection

Let D_h be the discrete divergence, G_h the discrete gradient, and L_h the
discrete Laplacian. A compatible scheme should satisfy:

D_h G_h = L_h

The discrete pressure equation is:

L_h p = (rho/Delta t) D_h u*

The corrected velocity is:

u^(n+1) = u* - (Delta t/rho) G_h p

Then:

D_h u^(n+1) approximately equals zero

The remaining divergence should be consistent with solver tolerance and numerical
round-off.

6.6 Pressure reference level

Only pressure differences affect the velocity correction. If p is a solution, then
p plus any constant is also a solution. Therefore pressure is determined only up to
an additive constant unless a reference pressure is imposed.

For periodic Poisson problems, the right-hand side must have zero spatial mean.
The zero Fourier mode is singular and is normally assigned a chosen reference value,
often zero.

======================================================================
7. PERIODIC POISSON SOLVER AND FOURIER THEORY
======================================================================

7.1 Why Fourier methods help

For a periodic domain, Fourier modes are eigenfunctions of constant-coefficient
derivative operators. A differential equation can therefore be transformed into
algebraic equations for each wave number.

For a continuous Fourier mode:

p(x) = p_hat exp(i k dot x)

The Laplacian acts as:

Laplacian(p) = -|k|^2 p

Thus the Poisson equation:

Laplacian(p) = rhs

becomes:

-|k|^2 p_hat = rhs_hat

For nonzero wave number:

p_hat = -rhs_hat / |k|^2

The zero wave number has eigenvalue zero and corresponds to the arbitrary pressure
constant. The mean of the right-hand side must be zero for a periodic solution.

7.2 Discrete periodic Laplacian

For a uniform 2D Cartesian grid, the five-point Laplacian is:

L_h(p)_ij =
[p_(i+1,j) - 2p_(i,j) + p_(i-1,j)]/h^2
+
[p_(i,j+1) - 2p_(i,j) + p_(i,j-1)]/h^2

Its Fourier eigenvalue is:

lambda(k_x,k_y) =
-4 sin^2(k_x h/2)/h^2
-4 sin^2(k_y h/2)/h^2

For each nonzero mode:

p_hat(k_x,k_y) = rhs_hat(k_x,k_y) / lambda(k_x,k_y)

Set the zero mode to the selected pressure reference, commonly zero.

The notebook uses this discrete eigenvalue in its Fourier Poisson demonstration.
This is an educational periodic solver, not the exact specialised fast Laplace
solver from Peskin (1977).

7.3 Periodic boundary condition limitation

A periodic domain connects one edge to the opposite edge. This is mathematically
convenient but is not automatically a realistic heart-domain boundary condition.
A realistic chamber or circulation model needs suitable inlet, outlet, wall, and
possibly source/sink conditions.

======================================================================
8. TIME DISCRETISATION AND NUMERICAL STABILITY
======================================================================

8.1 Time stepping

The continuous equations evolve in time. A numerical solver advances from time
t^n to t^(n+1):

t^(n+1) = t^n + Delta t

The time step Delta t must be small enough to resolve the physical motion and to
maintain numerical stability for the selected discretisation.

8.2 Explicit diffusion restriction

For an explicit treatment of viscous diffusion on a uniform grid, a typical
two-dimensional stability restriction scales as:

Delta t <= h^2 / (4 nu)

The exact constant depends on the scheme and grid. This is a stability estimate,
not a universal rule for all solvers.

8.3 Convective CFL restriction

A typical explicit advection condition is:

CFL = U Delta t / h

For multidimensional Cartesian flow, a common estimate is:

Delta t * (|u|/h_x + |v|/h_y) <= C_max

where C_max depends on the numerical method. The time step should be reduced when
flow speeds increase or grid spacing decreases.

8.4 Elastic stiffness restriction

A stiff spring network can introduce fast mechanical time scales. Increasing
spring stiffness can require a smaller explicit time step. An implicit or
semi-implicit boundary solve may be needed to avoid severe time-step restrictions.

8.5 Accuracy versus stability

A simulation can be numerically stable and still inaccurate. Accuracy must be
assessed through:
- Grid refinement.
- Time-step refinement.
- Comparison with analytical or manufactured solutions.
- Conservation checks.
- Residual monitoring.
- Sensitivity to model assumptions and parameters.

======================================================================
9. COUPLED IMMERSED-BOUNDARY TIME STEP
======================================================================

The fluid and boundary equations are coupled. A conceptual time step is:

1. Start with fluid velocity u^n and boundary configuration X^n.
2. Evaluate boundary mechanical forces from X^n or from a trial updated position.
3. Spread Lagrangian boundary forces to the Eulerian grid.
4. Advance the fluid momentum equation to obtain provisional velocity u*.
5. Solve the pressure Poisson equation.
6. Correct the fluid velocity to obtain a divergence-free u^(n+1).
7. Interpolate the fluid velocity to the Lagrangian boundary markers.
8. Update boundary positions.
9. Repeat for the next time step.

For an explicit boundary update:

X^(n+1) = X^n + Delta t * U_b^n

where U_b^n is the interpolated fluid velocity at the boundary.

This sequence is a conceptual operator split. The exact ordering and whether
boundary positions are treated explicitly or implicitly depend on the chosen IB
scheme.

9.1 Why nonlinear solves can appear

Boundary forces depend on the boundary configuration. But the boundary configuration
at the next time level depends on fluid velocity, which itself depends on the
boundary force. This creates an implicit coupling.

A generic nonlinear system can be written:

R(X^(n+1)) = 0

where R is the residual of the coupled boundary update.

Newton's method solves for a correction Delta X:

J(X_m) Delta X = -R(X_m)

X_(m+1) = X_m + Delta X

where J is the Jacobian matrix:

J = partial R / partial X

The solve repeats until the residual or update is sufficiently small.

A fixed boundary-link topology can produce a sparse Jacobian because each marker
interacts directly only with nearby connected markers. Sparse linear algebra can
therefore reduce computational cost.

Peskin (1977) discusses a nonlinear fixed-point/boundary configuration calculation
and exploitation of the link structure. A simple explicit spring update in this
teaching repository is not equivalent to reproducing that complete nonlinear solve.

======================================================================
10. HEART-SPECIFIC PHYSIOLOGY AND FUTURE MODEL EXTENSIONS
======================================================================

10.1 Cardiac cycle

A simplified cardiac cycle has four broad phases:
- Ventricular filling.
- Isovolumetric contraction.
- Ventricular ejection.
- Isovolumetric relaxation.

The ventricle changes volume as the myocardium contracts and relaxes. Pressure
differences open and close the valves, directing flow.

A two-dimensional IB model can represent a moving chamber wall and deforming valve
leaflets, but realistic valve opening and closure require suitable geometry,
mechanics, and pressure/flow boundary conditions.

10.2 Valves

A valve leaflet is a deformable immersed structure. Its motion is influenced by:
- Fluid pressure difference across the leaflet.
- Fluid shear and hydrodynamic loading.
- Leaflet elasticity and bending resistance.
- Attachment constraints at the annulus or hinge.
- Contact between leaflets, if closure is modelled.

A simple spring network does not automatically capture leaflet bending, contact,
or realistic tissue anisotropy.

10.3 Active myocardium

The myocardium is not purely passive elastic tissue. Muscle activation generates
active tension that changes over the cardiac cycle.

A later model may express total tissue force as:

f_total = f_passive + f_active + f_viscous

The active term may depend on time, activation state, fibre direction, and stretch.
No specific active-tension law is currently implemented in the initial teaching
model.

10.4 Source and sink flow

The original Peskin heart model includes source/sink treatment associated with
pulmonary venous return and changing enclosed volume in a periodic computational
setting. This is a specialised modelling component, not merely an arbitrary
numerical correction.

A future implementation must define:
- Where fluid enters and leaves.
- The physical meaning of each source or sink.
- The prescribed or coupled flow rate.
- How mass balance is maintained.
- How the treatment interacts with pressure and the computational domain.

Do not add source/sink terms until the closed-domain fluid solver and its mass
balance have been tested.

10.5 Reduced-order alternative

A full IB simulation resolves spatial flow and deforming geometry. A reduced-order
heart or pump model instead represents chambers, valves, and vessels through
lumped pressure, volume, resistance, inertance, and compliance relationships.

These approaches answer different questions. The IB solver investigates spatial
fluid-structure behaviour; a reduced-order model is cheaper for whole-circulation
or system-level dynamics.

======================================================================
11. BOUNDARY CONDITIONS AND DOMAIN CHOICES
======================================================================

The governing equations are not a complete problem until boundary and initial
conditions are specified.

Possible fluid boundary conditions include:

No-slip stationary wall:
u = 0

Moving no-slip wall:
u = U_wall

Prescribed velocity inlet:
u = u_in(t)

Prescribed pressure outlet:
p = p_out(t)

Periodic boundary:
u(x + L) = u(x), and corresponding periodic conditions for pressure and other
fields.

The appropriate choice depends on the physical question and geometry. A periodic
box is convenient for numerical experiments but should not be mistaken for a
complete physiological circulation boundary condition.

Initial conditions include:
- Initial velocity field u(x,0).
- Initial pressure reference or pressure field.
- Initial boundary marker positions X(s,0).
- Initial tissue state or link rest lengths.

======================================================================
12. NUMERICAL VERIFICATION AND PHYSICAL VALIDATION
======================================================================

A visually plausible flow plot does not prove that a solver is correct.

Verification asks: are the equations being solved correctly by the implementation?

Validation asks: are the equations and assumptions an adequate representation of
the physical system of interest?

12.1 Unit and dimensional checks

Check that all terms in each equation have matching units. In particular:
- Momentum equation terms: N/m^3.
- Velocity: m/s.
- Pressure: Pa.
- Dynamic viscosity: Pa s.
- Density: kg/m^3.
- Kinematic viscosity: m^2/s.
- Boundary and fluid forces must use consistent definitions and units.

12.2 Continuity check

Monitor the divergence field:

div(u)

A useful scalar diagnostic is RMS divergence:

D_RMS = sqrt( mean( [div(u)]^2 ) )

It should be small relative to the characteristic velocity-gradient scale and should
decrease appropriately as the pressure solve is tightened or the discretisation is
refined.

12.3 Poisson residual

For the discrete pressure equation:

r_p = L_h(p) - rhs

Monitor a norm such as:

||r_p||_2

A small residual indicates that the discrete Poisson equation has been solved
accurately. It does not by itself prove that the fluid model is physically correct.

12.4 Force conservation

Compare the total Lagrangian force with the integral of the spread Eulerian force:

F_total,IB = sum_k f_k Delta s_k

F_total,grid = sum_ij F_ij h^2

These should agree to the accuracy expected from the quadrature and kernel.

12.5 Internal force balance

For an isolated spring link:

F_A + F_B = 0

For a closed network, the sum of internal link forces should also be zero, up to
round-off and implementation details, if every link is assembled with equal and
opposite endpoint contributions.

12.6 Energy and work

For a spring link:

E_s = (1/2) k_s (L - L_0)^2

Track boundary elastic energy over time. In a passive system, energy changes should
be explainable by work exchanged with the fluid, viscous dissipation, and numerical
error. If active muscle forcing is later added, it can inject energy.

12.7 Grid and time refinement

Run the same test with:
- Smaller grid spacing h.
- Smaller time step Delta t.
- The same physical domain and physical parameters.

A convergent method should show systematic behaviour as h and Delta t decrease.
Compare quantitative quantities, not only images.

12.8 Manufactured and analytical tests

Before heart geometry, test:
- Constant velocity interpolation.
- Force spreading from one marker.
- A single spring link.
- A closed spring loop.
- Viscous diffusion of a known velocity profile.
- Periodic Poisson equation with a known manufactured solution.
- Projection of a deliberately divergent velocity field.
- Flow past a simple fixed or prescribed moving boundary.

Each component should have a test with a known expected result.

12.9 Diagnostics to log during simulation

At each time step, record:
- Time.
- Maximum fluid speed.
- RMS divergence.
- Maximum absolute divergence.
- Pressure Poisson residual.
- Total spread force.
- Net internal boundary force.
- Boundary elastic energy.
- Boundary displacement.
- Time-step and grid parameters.

These logs help distinguish physical behaviour from numerical instability.

======================================================================
13. IMPLEMENTATION ORDER FOR THIS REPOSITORY
======================================================================

The recommended progression is deliberately incremental.

Stage 1 — Fluid equations and grid
- Define the 2D Cartesian grid.
- Implement discrete gradient, divergence, and Laplacian operators.
- Test each operator on simple fields.
- Implement a basic viscous/advection predictor.

Stage 2 — Pressure projection
- Derive the discrete pressure equation from the actual discrete velocity update.
- Implement the Poisson solver.
- Correct velocity.
- Verify that divergence decreases.
- Ensure the gradient, divergence, and Laplacian are compatible.

Stage 3 — Boundary representation
- Create Lagrangian marker points.
- Define marker connectivity.
- Store marker positions and link rest lengths.
- Plot the boundary independently of the fluid.

Stage 4 — Boundary mechanics
- Implement one spring link.
- Verify equal-and-opposite forces.
- Extend to a closed loop or simple membrane.
- Track elastic energy.

Stage 5 — Delta-kernel transfer
- Implement force spreading.
- Implement velocity interpolation.
- Verify partition of unity, constant preservation, and force conservation.
- Check units and quadrature scaling.

Stage 6 — One-way moving-boundary test
- Prescribe simple boundary motion.
- Spread boundary forces to the fluid.
- Solve fluid flow.
- Interpolate velocity for diagnostics.
- Keep prescribed motion distinct from predicted motion.

Stage 7 — Two-way coupling
- Let boundary points move with interpolated fluid velocity.
- Recompute tissue forces as the boundary changes.
- Monitor stability and conservation.
- Reduce the time step or use implicit coupling if required.

Stage 8 — Heart geometry and physiology
- Introduce a simplified chamber wall.
- Add a valve leaflet and attachment constraints.
- Add more realistic tissue mechanics.
- Add activation and source/sink circulation treatment only after the simpler model
  is stable and validated.
